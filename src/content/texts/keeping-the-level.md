There is a particular kind of work that only exists while someone is paying attention to
it, and *Standing Water* is one of them. Four pumps run continuously beneath a plane of
water thirty-eight millimetres deep. They are not there to move it. They are there to
undo the movement the building keeps putting into it — the tram on Nordre Frihavnsgade,
the ventilation cycling on the hour, the footfall of anyone who walks in.

This is worth stating plainly because it inverts the usual relationship between an
installation and its room. Most work of this kind is placed *in* a space. This one is
calibrated *against* one. Move it and it is not the same work with a new address; it is
a different set of numbers doing the same job. At Magasin B in 2019 the floor fell
twenty-two millimetres from one end to the other and the entire first day of installation
went on a timber sub-frame nobody would ever see. At Verkstad Nord two years later the
concrete was level within three millimetres and the plane could grow to five and a half
metres across, which needed six pumps instead of four.

Neither of those facts is visible in a photograph, which is the difficulty the artist has
been unusually willing to admit. The documentation of this work states what it cannot
carry: the sound, which is constant and audible from the stairwell, and the stillness
itself, which only reads as stillness when a body moves past it. A still image of a still
surface is just a grey rectangle.

What survives instead is the record — the room measured, the pumps counted, the floor's
fall written down. That is a strange thing for an artwork to leave behind, and it is
closer to what the work actually is than any single view of it.
